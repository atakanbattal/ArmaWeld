Types of Welding Electrodes: Rutile, Basic (E7018) and Cellulosic

2026-05-20 6 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

A covered electrode consists of a core wire and the coating around it. The coating stabilises the arc, produces shielding gas and slag, and adds alloying elements to the weld metal. The main difference between electrodes lies in this coating, and it directly determines ease of welding, mechanical properties and hydrogen risk.

How to Read the ISO 2560-A Code

Example: E 42 4 B 42 H5

  • E: covered electrode.
  • 42: minimum weld metal yield strength about 420 MPa.
  • 4: at least 47 J impact energy at −40 °C.
  • B: basic coating.
  • 4: recovery and current type (here 105–125% recovery, DC).
  • 2: welding positions (all except vertical down).
  • H5: at most 5 ml/100 g diffusible hydrogen in the weld metal.

Alloyed electrodes add a composition symbol, e.g. “E 46 3 1Ni B …”.

The AWS A5.1 Code

In the AWS system E7018 reads: E electrode, 70 about 70 ksi (≈490 MPa) tensile strength, 1 all positions, 8 basic iron-powder coating and current type. E6013 is 60 ksi (≈420 MPa), all positions, rutile coating. AWS and ISO classes do not map one to one; select according to the system the specification calls for.

Which Electrode When?

  • Basic (E7018 / E 42 4 B): load-bearing structures, thick parts, S355 and above, work with impact requirements, joints with high crack risk. Generally the preferred class for EN 1090 EXC2 and higher structural work.
  • Rutile (E6013 / E 42 0 RR): light structures, thin sheet, work where appearance matters and toughness requirements are low.
  • Cellulosic (E6010): mainly pipeline root runs; rarely needed in general fabrication.

Diameter and Current Range

A practical rule is about 30–40 A per millimetre of electrode diameter. The exact value comes from the manufacturer’s data sheet and the WPS.

Hydrogen and Baking

The low-hydrogen property of basic electrodes only holds if the coating stays dry. A coating that picks up moisture carries hydrogen into the weld metal and raises cold-cracking risk, especially in high-strength and thick steels. Basic electrodes are therefore baked to the manufacturer’s instructions (typically a few hours at about 300–350 °C) and kept in heated quivers until use. Vacuum-packed electrodes are used within the stated time after opening. Together with preheat, hydrogen control is the core step in preventing cracks (see preheat calculation).

Common Mistakes

  • Leaving basic electrodes out and using them the next day without re-baking.
  • Using rutile electrodes on structural work with impact requirements.
  • A mismatch between the coating type on the welder’s certificate and the electrode used (see ISO 9606-1).
  • Not fully removing slag between passes, the most common cause of slag inclusions.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between rutile and basic electrodes?

Rutile electrodes are easy to use and give a neat bead but have limited toughness and hydrogen control. Basic electrodes are low hydrogen with high toughness and crack resistance, preferred for load-bearing and thick work.

What does E7018 mean?

A low-hydrogen electrode with about 70 ksi (≈490 MPa) tensile strength, usable in all positions, with a basic iron-powder coating.

Why are basic electrodes baked?

The coating absorbs moisture, which enters the weld metal as hydrogen and raises cold-cracking risk. Baking and heated storage prevent this.

What current for a 3.2 mm electrode?

Usually about 90–130 A; the exact value follows the manufacturer’s data sheet and the WPS.
