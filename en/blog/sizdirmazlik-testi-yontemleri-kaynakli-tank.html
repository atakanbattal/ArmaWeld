Leak Testing Welded Tanks and Housings: Which Method, When?

2026-10-05 7 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

Hydraulic oil tank, fuel tank, compressed-air receiver, water reservoir or a closed chassis section: the shared expectation is that the welds never leak. A leak found in the field means removing, cleaning and rewelding the part. That is why the specification should state the leak test method and acceptance criterion clearly.

Methods and When to Use Them

  • Bubble test: the part is filled with low-pressure air and the welds are wetted with foaming solution; bubbles form at a leak. Quick and cheap, sufficient for most hydraulic and fuel tanks. Pressure is set by design and a relief valve must be used.
  • Paraffin-chalk test: one side is coated with chalk, paraffin is applied to the other; penetration stains the chalk. Used on open tanks without pressure; needs a dwell time.
  • Vacuum box: for welds accessible from one side only (e.g. tank floors), a transparent box is placed over the wetted weld and a vacuum drawn.
  • Hydrostatic test: the part is filled with water and pressurised. It shows tightness and strength and is standard for pressure vessels. Water stores little energy, so it is far safer than a pneumatic test. Drying and corrosion protection follow.
  • Pneumatic pressure test: with air or gas; compressed gas stores a lot of energy, so it is used only where hydrostatic testing is impossible and with strict safety measures.
  • Helium leak test: a sensitive method able to measure very small leaks, used in refrigeration, vacuum and gas systems. Requires special equipment and expertise.

The Sensitivity Question

Standards such as ISO 20484 describe method sensitivity. A bubble test suits a hydraulic tank, while a refrigerant circuit may need helium. An over-sensitive method adds cost; an inadequate one leads to leaks in service. The right choice depends on medium, pressure and acceptable leak rate.

Weld-Related Causes of Leaks

  • Crater cracks and lack of fusion at weld starts and stops.
  • Porosity chains, especially on dirty or oily surfaces.
  • Incomplete root penetration in corner joints.
  • Nodes where welds intersect.

Visual inspection before testing and, where needed, penetrant testing reduce these risks.

What to Write in the Specification

Test method, pressure and hold time, acceptance criterion (no visible leak or a defined leak rate), whether testing is before or after painting, test report and marking on the part.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which test suits a hydraulic tank?

Usually a low-pressure air bubble test; the specification governs.

Why is hydrostatic testing safer?

Water is incompressible and stores little energy; air tests carry a burst risk.

When is helium testing needed?

Where very low leak rates are required: refrigeration, vacuum and gas systems.

Test before painting?

Usually yes; paint can temporarily seal small leaks.
