Manual Metal Arc Welding (MMA / 111): When Is It a Better Choice Than MIG/MAG?

2026-05-26 6 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

Manual metal arc welding (MMA, SMAW, process 111 in ISO 4063) is one of the oldest and simplest arc welding processes. In workshops it has largely given way to MIG/MAG for productivity, but in some situations it is still the right and safest choice. This article explains where MMA stands out, where it falls short and what determines quality.

How It Works

The arc between electrode and workpiece melts both the core wire and the coating. As the coating burns it produces shielding gas, leaves a slag layer over the pool and adds alloying elements to the weld metal. No separate gas cylinder or wire feeder is needed; a constant-current power source and an electrode holder are enough.

Current Type and Polarity

  • DC+ (electrode positive): the most common connection for basic electrodes; deep penetration and a stable arc.
  • DC− (electrode negative): with some rutile electrodes and on thin sheet for less penetration.
  • AC: where arc blow is a problem, with electrodes suited to AC.

The polarity is stated in the electrode data sheet and the WPS (see types of electrodes).

Where MMA Stands Out

  • Site and outdoor work: wind disperses MIG/MAG shielding gas; with MMA the shielding comes from the coating, so it is far more wind tolerant.
  • Hard-to-reach areas: an electrode holder reaches narrow spots where a torch and hose package cannot.
  • Repair welding: short welds, in-place repairs and small numbers of parts with quick set-up.
  • Roots or special passes on thick parts: basic electrodes are a reliable option when low hydrogen and high toughness are required.
  • Portability: the equipment is light and can run from a generator at remote locations.

Limitations

  • Low productivity: the arc-on time ratio is low; changing electrodes, removing slag and stub ends cost time and material. Deposition is generally well below MIG/MAG.
  • Interpass cleaning: slag must be fully removed after each pass, otherwise slag inclusions form.
  • Dependence on welder skill: arc length, electrode angle and travel speed are entirely in the welder’s hands.
  • Thin sheet: below 2 mm the risk of burn-through and distortion is high; TIG or MIG/MAG is usually better.

For series work in the workshop MIG/MAG is almost always more economical (see MIG/MAG welding). MMA is valuable as a complementary process for certain welds, repairs and special cases.

Typical Defects and Causes

  • Slag inclusions: insufficient interpass cleaning, wrong electrode angle.
  • Porosity: damp electrodes, too long an arc, start porosity with basic electrodes.
  • Arc strikes: striking the arc outside the weld; can initiate cracks in high-strength steels.
  • Undercut: too much current or wrong electrode angle.

For detecting and repairing defects see our article on weld defects and repair.

Qualification

For structural welds made by MMA, the welder must be certified to ISO 9606-1 for process 111 and the electrode type used (rutile, basic, etc.) must be within the certificate range. The procedure is defined in a WPS and qualified by a WPQR where required.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is MMA welding?

Manual arc welding with a covered electrode (SMAW, ISO 4063 process 111). Shielding comes from gas and slag produced by the burning coating.

Is stick welding or MIG/MAG better?

MIG/MAG is more productive for series work in the workshop. On windy sites, in restricted access and for repairs, stick welding can be more practical and reliable.

Which polarity for basic electrodes?

Mostly DC+ (electrode positive). The exact information is in the electrode data sheet and the WPS.

Can thin sheet be welded with MMA?

It is possible, but below 2 mm the risk of burn-through and distortion is high; TIG or MIG/MAG is usually better.
