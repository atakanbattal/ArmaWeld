How to Design Welded Parts for Hot-Dip Galvanizing: Holes, Welding and Steel Selection

2026-11-16 8 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

Hot-dip galvanizing coats a welded steel part by dipping it into a bath of molten zinc at about 450 °C. On a well-designed part the coating lasts for decades. On a poorly designed one the zinc cannot drain, closed volumes can burst, the welds come out with black spots or the part distorts. Almost all of these problems can be prevented at the drawing stage. In this article we have collected practical rules for welded parts to be galvanized, based on EN ISO 14713-2 and EN ISO 1461.

1. Vent and Drain Holes

Before galvanizing the part is cleaned in acid baths and then dipped into zinc. Liquids and air entering hollow sections and box sections must have a way out. Otherwise:

  • Trapped liquid turns to steam and builds up pressure; this can make the part burst and injure people.
  • Inner surfaces with trapped air are not coated and rust from the inside.
  • Zinc cannot drain, so the part becomes heavier and more expensive than necessary.

Practical rules:

  • Holes are placed at both ends of the section, at diagonally opposite corners and as close to the corner as possible, taking into account the angle at which the part will be dipped.
  • Hole diameter is chosen from the tables in EN ISO 14713-2 according to section size; even on small sections, holes below 10 mm are not recommended.
  • Corners of flanges, end plates and stiffeners are cropped so that zinc does not collect in the corner.
  • Where large overlapping joints are sealed with an all-round weld, a separate vent hole is drilled for the air between the plates.

2. Welding and Post-Weld Cleaning

The acid bath removes rust and mill scale but does not remove weld slag. Slag, spatter and burnt anti-spatter spray left on the weld show up as black uncoated spots.

  • With stick and flux-cored welding the slag must be removed completely by mechanical means (brushing, chipping, blasting).
  • If anti-spatter spray is used, choose a silicone-free product.
  • Use continuous welds instead of intermittent fillet welds; acid enters the narrow gaps between intermittent welds, does not dry and later causes rust staining.
  • Welds must be free of porosity; acid trapped in pores causes blisters in the coating.

3. Steel Selection: Silicon and Phosphorus

The chemical composition of the steel directly affects the coating thickness and appearance. EN ISO 14713-2 classifies steels by their silicon (Si) and phosphorus (P) content:

Si contentExpected coating
≤ 0.04% (P < 0.02%)Normal thickness, bright and even appearance
0.04% – 0.14%Sandelin range: risk of a very thick, grey and brittle coating
0.14% – 0.25%Normal to slightly thicker, usually matt appearance
> 0.25%Thick, matt grey coating

If plates from different heats and thicknesses are used in one part, colour differences can occur. Where appearance matters, specify suitability for hot-dip galvanizing in the steel order and check the Si and P values on the 3.1 certificate. For high-strength and heavily cold-formed steels the risk of liquid metal embrittlement should also be assessed.

4. Preventing Distortion

  • Design the part as symmetrically as possible; sections with heavy welding on one side distort in the bath.
  • Avoid joining very thin and very thick plates in the same part. Thin, wide sheets should be supported with stiffeners.
  • Plan the welding sequence to balance welding stresses. Parts with high residual stress relax and change shape at 450 °C.
  • The part must fit in the galvanizing kettle. Parts larger than the kettle can be double-dipped, but that means extra cost and a risk at the overlap zone. Confirm dimensions with the galvanizer at quotation stage.

5. Coating Thickness: EN ISO 1461

EN ISO 1461 sets minimum coating thicknesses by steel thickness. For example, for steel thicker than 6 mm the local minimum is 70 µm and the mean minimum 85 µm; for 3–6 mm it is 55 µm local and 70 µm mean. If a thicker coating is needed, it must be stated separately on the drawing or order.

6. Notes Your Drawing Should Include

  • "Hot-dip galvanized to EN ISO 1461" and any special coating thickness
  • Position and diameter of vent and drain holes
  • Lifting points or hanging holes
  • Areas to be kept free of zinc, such as threads, bearing seats and slip-resistant faying surfaces
  • A duplex system note if the part is to be painted after galvanizing
  • Permanent part marking: hard-stamped numbers on the steel instead of paint markers

Frequently Asked Questions

Which welding process suits parts to be galvanized?

MAG (135) and TIG (141), which leave no slag, need the least cleaning. Stick and flux-cored welding can also be used, but the slag must be removed completely.

Can you weld after galvanizing?

Yes, but the zinc in the weld zone burns off and the fumes are harmful. After welding the area must be repaired with zinc-rich paint or thermal spraying. Where possible, all welding should be finished before galvanizing.

How long does a galvanized coating last?

Coating life depends on the corrosivity of the environment and the coating thickness. In rural and urban environments it can last for decades without maintenance; in coastal and industrial environments life is shorter. EN ISO 14713-1 gives guidance for this estimate.
