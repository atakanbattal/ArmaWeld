Quality Assurance Agreement (QAA) with a Welding Supplier: What Should It Contain?

2025-12-22 8 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

A purchase contract covers price, delivery dates and payment terms. A quality assurance agreement (QAA, called QSV in German-speaking countries) defines what "the right part" is and what happens when something goes wrong. Because quality in welded parts depends heavily on process control, a well-written QAA prevents most disputes with the supplier before they start.

QAA versus Technical Specification

The technical specification and drawing describe a specific part: material, dimensions, weld class, surface treatment. The QAA sets the framework that applies to all parts: the supplier's quality system, notification duties, the non-conformity process, records and audits. The two complement each other; the QAA refers to part-specific requirements rather than repeating them.

Change Notification: the Most Overlooked Clause

In welded parts, changes that look minor can strongly affect the result. The QAA should require prior notification and, where necessary, approval for:

  • A change of welding process, filler metal or shielding gas.
  • A change of material supplier or steel mill.
  • A change of production site or critical equipment.
  • A change of outsourced process partner (e.g. galvaniser or painter).
  • Process changes that affect distortion, such as welding sequence or fixture changes.

Non-Conformities and Cost

Who does what when a problem occurs should be written down in advance: the supplier's notification time, quarantine of suspect stock, sorting and repair rules, the format and timing of root cause analysis (see NCR and 8D). Cost clauses should be realistic; a serious supplier will struggle to sign a QAA with unlimited liability, which drags out negotiations.

Record Retention

How long welding records, NDT reports, material certificates and dimensional reports are kept depends on the sector and product life. In structural steel and pressure equipment this period is usually long. Keeping records digital and searchable allows a quick answer to a question that arrives years later (see digital traceability in welding).

Tips for a Good QAA

  • Adapt the generic template to your parts; an automotive QAA does not fit heavy steel fabrication one to one.
  • Refer to standards, but state clearly which level is required (e.g. ISO 5817 level C).
  • Review it together with the supplier; clauses that cannot be applied stay on paper.
  • Link it to project documents such as the inspection and test plan (see ITP).

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a quality assurance agreement (QAA)?

An agreement between customer and supplier that sets the rules for quality system, notification, non-conformities, records and audits.

Is a QAA the same as a technical specification?

No. A specification describes a specific part; the QAA sets the quality framework for all parts.

Which changes must be notified in advance?

Welding process, filler and gas, material supplier, production site, outsourced process partner and process changes that affect distortion.

How should cost clauses in a QAA look?

Realistic and clearly limited; unlimited liability makes negotiations difficult.
