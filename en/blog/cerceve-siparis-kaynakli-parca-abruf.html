Framework Orders and Call-Offs for Welded Parts: How to Set Them Up

2026-09-07 7 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

When a welded part moves from prototype to series, quoting and ordering every batch separately wears out both buyer and supplier. Most European machine builders then switch to a framework (blanket) order with call-offs. Set up properly, it fixes the price, shortens lead time and lets both sides plan.

What Is a Framework Order?

An order in which total quantity, unit price and delivery terms are agreed in advance for a period, usually 12 months. Actual deliveries are triggered by call-offs the buyer sends during that period, so there is no renegotiation for each delivery.

What a Framework Order Contains

  • Part number and drawing revision,
  • Period and total (forecast) quantity,
  • Unit price and validity conditions,
  • Minimum and maximum call-off quantity,
  • Time from call-off to delivery,
  • Safety stock or quantity of semi-finished parts held ready,
  • Material price clause,
  • Packaging, Incoterms and documentation requirements,
  • Liability for remaining material and parts at the end of the term.

Forecast and Frozen Zone

A good framework order relies on the buyer sharing a rolling forecast, usually split into three zones:

ZoneMeaningTypical use
Firm (frozen)Quantity and date no longer changeBatches in production or with material already cut
Material releaseSupplier may buy material, production not yet startedPeriod in which plate and sections are ordered
ForecastFor planning onlyCapacity planning, not binding

The length of each zone depends on the part and on material lead times. The material release zone matters so the supplier can order special-grade plate or long-lead sections.

Safety Stock and Semi-Finished Parts

The most effective way to shorten call-off lead time is for the supplier to hold a quantity of finished parts or cut and bent blanks. Its cost and liability must be written into the framework order: does the buyer commit to taking this stock at the end of the term?

Price and Material Clause

Steel prices can move significantly within a year. Two approaches are common:

  • Fixed price: the supplier prices in the risk; suitable for short terms.
  • Material-indexed price: the unit price is split into material and labour; the material share is updated at set intervals against an agreed index.

In the indexed model, state the index source, update frequency and a threshold (for example no update below a certain percentage change).

Revision Management

Drawing revisions are inevitable in series production. The framework order should define:

  • From which call-off a revision applies,
  • What happens to stock and blanks of the old revision,
  • Who pays if a fixture or WPS change is needed,
  • A new first article approval where needed.

Fixtures and Tooling

Ownership, maintenance and return on termination of welding fixtures built for series production should be set out in the framework order or a separate tooling agreement (see welding fixtures for series production).

Performance Indicators

  • On-time in-full rate (OTIF),
  • Actual call-off-to-delivery time,
  • Nonconformities per batch and 8D closure time,
  • Completeness of documentation.

Reviewing these together every quarter catches problems before they grow.

Common Mistakes

  • Expecting short lead times without sharing a forecast,
  • Not stating end-of-term liability,
  • Announcing revisions by email without linking them to call-offs,
  • Very small minimum call-offs that repeat set-up cost on every batch.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does a framework order differ from a normal order?

A normal order covers a single delivery. A framework order fixes quantity and price for a period; deliveries are made through call-offs during that period.

What does frozen zone mean?

The part of the forecast where quantity and date no longer change because production has started or material has been cut.

Who owns remaining stock at the end of the term?

This must be written into the framework order. Usually the buyer commits to taking released material and agreed safety stock.

What if material prices rise?

With a fixed price the risk lies with the supplier. In an indexed model the material share is updated against the agreed index.
