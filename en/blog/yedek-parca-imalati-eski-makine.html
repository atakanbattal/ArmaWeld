Welded Spare Parts for Older Machines: What to Do When the Manufacturer Is Gone

2026-01-14 7 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

It is not unusual for a well-built machine to run for thirty years. The trouble starts the day a frame arm, a support bracket or a hopper wears out or cracks: the machine builder no longer exists, the part is not in any catalogue, or the lead time is measured in months. Welded parts are the easiest to remake in this situation, and with the right approach they can often be made more durable than the original.

Starting Point: What Do You Have?

SituationApproach
Original drawing availableReview against current standards and produce directly
No drawing, part intactMeasure the part, prepare drawing and 3D model
No drawing, part damagedUse the undamaged side, a matching part or the mounting dimensions as reference
No part, only the mounting locationMeasure interfaces and design a new part to the function

Callipers and a tape measure are often enough; for complex geometry, 3D scanning can be coordinated with partners (see reverse engineering).

Identifying the Material Correctly

If the material of an old part is unknown, there are a few routes: likely steel grades based on the machine's era and origin, on-site hardness testing, or chemical analysis in a laboratory. Choosing a welding procedure without knowing the material is risky; older steels in particular can have a high carbon equivalent and need preheat. Choosing a current, certified steel for the new part provides traceability (see EN 10204 3.1 certificate).

Understand Why It Failed

If a part cracked after thirty years, it may be fatigue, and making the same part the same way is probably enough. But if it breaks every few years, there is a problem in design or use. Examine the location and surface of the crack and look for causes such as sharp corners, weld toe terminations or an insufficient section (see fatigue in welded joints).

Improvement Opportunities

  • Wear plates or a harder material on worn surfaces.
  • A corner radius, stiffener or relocated weld where the crack started.
  • Drain holes, galvanising or a better paint system where corrosion appeared.
  • Bolted connections or slotted holes that make fitting easier.

Changes must not upset the mounting dimensions or how the part interacts with the rest of the machine. An improvement that changes function should also be assessed for machine safety.

CE and Liability

Making a spare part to match the original usually does not change the machine's conformity. But if a significant change is made to a load-bearing, guarding or safety-related part, the machine owner needs to assess it (see new Machinery Regulation). In areas such as lifting equipment, approval by a competent person may be required.

Speed and Records

With spare parts, line downtime often matters more than price. What speeds things up: clear photos and dimensions, standard material in stock, simple fixturing and quick decisions. Once the part has been made, its drawing, WPS and material should be recorded; when the same part is needed years later, lead time drops to days.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an old part without a drawing be remade?

Yes. A drawing and model are prepared from the existing part or the mounting location, then the part is produced.

How is the material of an old part identified?

By estimating from era and origin, on-site hardness testing or chemical analysis in a laboratory.

Can a spare part be made more durable than the original?

Often yes, with improvements such as wear plates, corner radii, stiffeners and better corrosion protection.

Does a spare part affect the machine's CE conformity?

A part matching the original usually does not; significant safety-related changes must be assessed by the machine owner.
