What Is a 3.1 Material Certificate? EN 10204 2.2, 3.1 and 3.2 Explained

2026-11-11 7 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

One of the most common lines in steel orders and welded fabrication specifications is "material with 3.1 certificate". It refers to EN 10204, the standard that defines the types of inspection documents supplied with metallic products. In this article we explain the difference between the document types, what to check on a 3.1 certificate and how the certificate is traced down to the part in welded fabrication.

EN 10204 Document Types

TypeDocument nameContentValidated by
2.1Declaration of complianceStates that the product complies with the order; no test resultsManufacturer
2.2Test reportNon-specific inspection results, i.e. values from similar productionManufacturer
3.1Inspection certificate 3.1Specific inspection results from the heat of the delivered productManufacturer's authorised representative, independent of manufacturing
3.2Inspection certificate 3.2Same content as 3.1, plus witnessing by an independent partyManufacturer's independent representative and the purchaser's representative or a designated body

Before the 2004 revision these documents were called 3.1.A, 3.1.B and 3.1.C. The phrase "3.1.B certificate" that you still hear corresponds to today's 3.1; 3.1.A and 3.1.C correspond to today's 3.2.

2.2 vs. 3.1

The key difference is whether the values belong to the product you actually received. A 2.2 report gives typical values from similar products. A 3.1 certificate gives values from samples of the delivered product's heat and is validated by an authorised person independent of the manufacturing department. That is why 3.1 is usually required for structural and load-bearing welded fabrication. EN 1090-2 also specifies which document type is needed depending on steel grade and execution class.

What to Check on a 3.1 Certificate

  1. Product standard and grade: for example EN 10025-2 S355J2+N. It must match the order exactly.
  2. Heat (cast) number: it must match the number marked on the plate or section.
  3. Dimensions and quantity: thickness, width, length and quantity must match the delivered material.
  4. Chemical composition: carbon and carbon equivalent (CEV) matter most; preheat requirements are calculated from them.
  5. Mechanical values: yield strength (ReH), tensile strength (Rm), elongation (A) and impact energy (Charpy KV) at the stated temperature.
  6. Validation: name, signature or electronic validation of the authorised person, and the date.

How the Certificate Is Traced to the Part

The certificate alone is not enough; what matters is knowing which part was cut from which plate. During cutting the heat number is transferred to the cut parts and recorded on the job order. That way you can show which certificate every plate of a finished chassis belongs to. Welding consumables (wire, electrodes, flux) have their own certificates, which can be added to the delivery file.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who issues a 3.1 certificate?

The steel mill that produced the material issues it, and it is validated by the mill's authorised representative who is independent of manufacturing. If you buy from a stockholder, the stockholder passes on a copy of the original mill certificate.

What is the difference between 3.1 and 3.2?

The content is the same; with 3.2, the purchaser's representative or a designated body also witnesses the inspection and validates the document. It costs more and takes longer, and is mostly required for pressure equipment and special projects.

Do welding wire and electrodes need certificates too?

In most structural and pressure equipment work, certificates for consumables are required as well. The project specification determines which document type is needed.
