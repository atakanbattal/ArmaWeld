Welded or Bolted? An Engineering Guide to Choosing the Connection

2026-03-09 6 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

When designing a steel part, the question "welded or bolted?" can affect cost and service life more than the weld size itself. The right answer is often "both": weld in the shop, bolt on site. This article compares the two methods technically and commercially.

Comparison Table

StiffnessHigh, continuous jointDepends on type; high with preloaded joints
WeightLighter; no splice plates or flangesConnection plates and bolts add weight

DismantlingNot possiblePossible; easier maintenance, replacement and transport

When Welding Wins

  • Tanks, silos, hoppers and pipe connections that must be tight.
  • Machine frames needing high stiffness and accurate geometry.
  • Vehicle bodies and chassis where weight matters.
  • Joints where force must flow continuously through the section.

When Bolting Wins

  • Splitting structures that exceed transport dimensions into modules.
  • Parts that will be removed or replaced during maintenance.
  • Projects where site welding is costly, risky or hard to control.
  • Parts joined after galvanizing or painting.
  • Splitting parts larger than the galvanizing bath.

Hybrid Approach: Weld in the Shop, Bolt on Site

In steel structures the most common and usually most economical solution is to fabricate sub-assemblies by welding in the shop and join them on site with bolts. Shop welding is done in a controlled environment, in flat position and with inspection; site connections become fast and repeatable. The critical point is keeping connection plates in the right position despite welding distortion. Correct weld sequence, fixtures and, where needed, drilling after welding reduce that risk.

Points to Watch in Bolted Joints

  • Hole clearance: EN 1090-2 normal clearance is 1 mm for M12-M14, 2 mm for M16-M24 and 3 mm for M27 and above.
  • Preloaded joints: slip-resistant and dynamically loaded joints use EN 14399 assemblies and a defined tightening method; faying surface preparation should also be specified.
  • Hole position tolerance: holes drilled before welding can shift through distortion; consider drilling critical holes after welding.

Points to Watch in Welded Joints

  • Size welds by calculation (see weld strength calculation).
  • Assess the FAT class of details under fluctuating load (see fatigue guide).
  • Provide vent holes in closed cavities of parts to be galvanized.

Five Questions for the Decision

  1. Can the part be transported and coated in one piece?
  2. Will it need dismantling during its service life?
  3. Is the joint under fluctuating load?
  4. Is there a tightness or hygiene requirement?
  5. Do site conditions allow weld quality to be controlled?

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a welded or bolted connection stronger?

Both carry the required load when designed correctly. Welding gives a stiffer, lighter joint; bolting offers dismantling and easier site assembly.

Which is better for fatigue?

Preloaded slip-resistant bolted joints often have a higher fatigue class. In welded details, the weld toe and root are the critical points.

How are galvanized parts joined?

Usually with bolts. Site welding damages the zinc coating and needs repair.

Why is shop welding with site bolting so common?

Welding is better and cheaper in a controlled shop; bolted site joints give fast, repeatable assembly.
