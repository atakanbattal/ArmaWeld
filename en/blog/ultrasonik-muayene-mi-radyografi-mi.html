Ultrasonic or Radiographic Testing? Choosing Between UT and RT for Welds

2025-12-18 7 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

There are two main methods for volumetric weld inspection: ultrasonic testing (UT) and radiographic testing (RT). Both look inside the weld, but they rely on different physics and find different defects with different success. If the specification names no method, or says "UT or RT", the right choice depends on geometry, thickness and the type of defect you are looking for.

The Basic Difference

UT sends high-frequency sound into the material and evaluates the echoes returning from discontinuities. RT projects an image of the weld onto film or a digital detector using X-rays or gamma rays. RT works like a photograph, UT like sonar.

Which Defect Does Each Find Best?

Defect typeUTRT
Crack (planar)Very good, orientation-dependentPoor unless parallel to the beam
Lack of sidewall fusionVery goodPoor to fair
Incomplete root penetrationGoodGood
Porosity (volumetric)FairVery good
Slag inclusionGoodVery good
Tungsten inclusionPoorVery good

In short: UT is stronger on planar, dangerous defects, RT on volumetric ones. Because cracks and lack of fusion are the most critical defects in fatigue-loaded structures, UT is often preferred there.

Thickness and Geometry

  • UT is usually efficient from 8 mm upward. EN ISO 17640 covers ferritic steel from 8 to 100 mm as standard; thinner material needs special techniques. The thicker the part, the bigger UT's advantage.
  • RT gives excellent results on thin material. With increasing thickness it needs a stronger source and longer exposure, and sensitivity drops.
  • RT needs access to both sides of the weld (source on one side, film on the other). UT can be done from one side.
  • RT images of fillet welds and T-joints are hard to interpret; UT suits these geometries better.

Material

In coarse-grained austenitic stainless and duplex welds, sound is scattered and conventional UT becomes difficult. These materials use RT or special UT techniques (TRL probes, PAUT). In carbon and low-alloy structural steels, UT works without problems.

Records and Traceability

RT's biggest advantage is a permanent image that can be reviewed years later. With conventional manual UT, the record is only what the operator writes in the report. Phased array (PAUT) and TOFD close this gap: scan data is stored digitally and can be re-evaluated.

Time, Cost and Safety

Time to resultImmediateAfter film processing or digital readout

Operator dependenceHighLower (at interpretation stage)

Because surrounding work must stop during RT, it affects the production plan. RT exposures are therefore often done outside shifts or in a separate area.

What Do the Standards Say?

EN 1090-2 does not prescribe the volumetric method; it asks for a choice based on thickness, joint type and material, and favours UT for thick butt welds. Pressure equipment (EN 13445, PED) and some customer specifications state the method explicitly. If the specification does not, clarify it in writing at quotation stage.

Practical Selection Guide

  • Thick butt weld (over 15 mm), fatigue loading, crack risk: UT
  • Thin-wall pipe and sheet butt welds, porosity control: RT
  • Permanent image record required: RT or PAUT
  • Austenitic stainless welds: RT or special UT
  • T-joints and corner joints: UT
  • Critical welds: both methods combined

Frequently Asked Questions

Are UT and RT used together?

Yes. On critical welds the methods complement each other: UT finds planar defects better, RT volumetric ones. Some specifications require both on certain welds.

What are PAUT and TOFD?

PAUT (phased array UT) steers the sound beam electronically with multi-element probes and produces a scan image. TOFD measures defect height from signals diffracted at the tips. Both leave a record and can replace RT.

Can thin sheet be tested with UT?

Conventional UT becomes difficult below 8 mm. RT or special high-frequency UT techniques are preferred on thin material.

Which method is cheaper?

UT is usually cheaper and faster on thick parts. RT can be more economical on thin-wall, repetitive pipe welds. Include production downtime in the total cost.
