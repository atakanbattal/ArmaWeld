Welding Stainless to Carbon Steel: 309L Filler, Dilution and Galvanic Corrosion

2026-05-01 6 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

A stainless hopper on a machine frame, a stainless connection plate on a carbon steel structure, carbon steel legs on a stainless food machine body: joining stainless to carbon steel is very common in fabrication. With the right filler and detail it is done safely; with the wrong filler, cracking and corrosion are inevitable.

The Real Issue: Dilution

Weld metal is a mix of the two melted parent metals and the wire. The carbon steel side adds iron and carbon to the pool; this mixing is called dilution and is typically 20–40%. With a standard stainless wire (e.g. 308L), dilution from the carbon steel lowers the chromium and nickel of the weld metal and hard, brittle martensite can form. A carbon steel wire, on the other hand, destroys corrosion resistance on the stainless side.

Why 309L?

309L wire (23 12 L to ISO 14343) contains more chromium and nickel than standard stainless wires. Despite dilution from the carbon steel, the weld metal stays austenitic with a little ferrite, a structure resistant to cracking and hot cracking. When joining molybdenum-bearing stainless (316L), 309LMo may be preferred.

The weld metal structure is predicted with the Schaeffler or DeLong diagram: the chromium and nickel equivalents of both parent metals and the wire are plotted for the dilution ratio, and the target point is in the austenite-plus-a-few-percent-ferrite region, not in the martensite or fully austenitic region.

Practical Rules

  • Keep dilution low: low heat input, narrow passes and directing the arc slightly to the stainless side reduce mixing from the carbon steel.
  • Buttering: on thick or critical joints, a 309L layer is first deposited on the carbon steel bevel face, then the joint is made. This also helps separate carbon-steel preheat or heat treatment needs from the stainless side.
  • Preheat: set by the thickness and carbon equivalent of the carbon steel side (see preheat calculation); the stainless side needs none.
  • Root protection: if the stainless inside is exposed to corrosion, back purge (see TIG welding of stainless).

Thermal Expansion Difference

Austenitic stainless expands about 40% more than carbon steel (roughly 16–17 µm/m·K versus 12 µm/m·K). This increases distortion during welding and creates fatigue stress in the joint under thermal cycling. Use a balanced sequence on long welds and allow for flexibility in the design of thermally cycled parts.

High-Temperature Service

In joints working continuously above about 370–400 °C, carbon migration from the carbon steel into the stainless side and the expansion difference can weaken a 309L joint over time. Nickel-based fillers (e.g. ERNiCr-3 class) are then preferred, since their expansion is closer to carbon steel.

Galvanic Corrosion and the Coating Detail

When stainless and carbon steel are in contact in a moist environment, the carbon steel side corrodes faster. The practical solution is to paint the carbon steel side and carry the paint over the weld a few centimetres onto the stainless. Leaving the stainless unpainted can cause an uncoated strip of carbon steel to corrode very quickly. Heat tint on the stainless side must also be removed (see pickling and passivation).

Frequently Asked Questions

Which filler joins stainless to carbon steel?

Usually 309L (ISO 14343 23 12 L), or 309LMo with 316L. For continuous service above about 370–400 °C, nickel-based fillers are preferred.

Can 308L be used to weld to carbon steel?

Not recommended. Dilution from the carbon steel can form hard, brittle martensite in the weld metal.

What does buttering do?

A 309L layer is first deposited on the carbon steel bevel face, controlling dilution so the joint can be made more safely.

How is galvanic corrosion prevented?

Paint the carbon steel side and carry the paint over the weld a few centimetres onto the stainless.
