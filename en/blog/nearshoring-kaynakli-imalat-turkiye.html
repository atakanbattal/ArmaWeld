Nearshoring: Why European Buyers Are Turning to Turkey for Welded Parts

2026-11-16 8 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

For many years European machine builders bought welded parts largely from distant countries for the lowest unit price. Freight swings, port congestion and long lead times in recent years have changed that calculation. Today many buyers ask not “what is the cheapest unit price?” but “what is the lowest total cost and the least risk?” Nearshoring, moving supply to geographically close countries, is one answer.

What Is Nearshoring?

Shifting production or sourcing to a country close to the buyer’s market. For Europe that usually means Eastern Europe, North Africa and Turkey. Unlike offshoring (moving far away) or reshoring (bringing it home), it keeps part of the cost advantage while reducing distance and risk.

Why Now?

  • Supply chain fragility: sea freight disruptions have caused long production stoppages.
  • Inventory cost: long transit ties up capital in transit and in warehouses.
  • Speed of design changes: revisions are hard to apply to parts that spend weeks at sea.
  • Reporting obligations: CBAM and supply chain laws require close, transparent communication with suppliers.

Turkey’s Strengths in Welded Fabrication

  • Geography: a few days by road to Central Europe; a small time difference allows same-day communication.
  • Customs union: there is a customs union with the EU for industrial goods; for iron and steel products, however, free trade rules and proof of origin apply (see A.TR, EUR.1 and origin).
  • Standards: European standards such as EN ISO 3834, EN 1090, ISO 9606 and ISO 9712 are widely applied by Turkish fabricators.
  • Strong supplier base: a wide network for cutting, bending, machining, galvanising and painting, which a welding shop can coordinate.
  • Flexibility: suited to small and medium batches, prototypes and frequent revisions.

A Nearshoring Project Step by Step

  1. Part selection: start with parts that are heavy, weld-intensive, ordered in volume and often revised.
  2. RFQ package: drawings, material, welding and inspection requirements, annual volume, packaging and Incoterms.
  3. Supplier assessment: certificates, sample WPS/WPQR, welder certificates, NDT capability, reference parts.
  4. Audit: on site or remote; welding coordination, traceability and quality records.
  5. First article approval: dimensional report, NDT, material certificates.
  6. Pilot batch: testing logistics, packaging and document flow.
  7. Series and improvement: regular review of delivery performance and quality indicators.

Who It Suits and Who It Doesn’t

Nearshoring pays off especially for medium-volume, large or heavy, weld-intensive and frequently revised parts. For very high-volume, standard, light parts the unit price advantage of distant sourcing may still decide. The right answer comes from a total cost calculation part by part.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between nearshoring and offshoring?

Offshoring moves production to a distant, usually low-cost country. Nearshoring picks a country close to the market, keeping part of the cost advantage while cutting distance and risk.

Are welded parts from Turkey duty-free?

There is a customs union with the EU for industrial goods, but iron and steel products may need origin rules and documents such as EUR.1. Assess by the product’s HS code.

Which parts suit nearshoring best?

Medium-volume, large or heavy, weld-intensive and frequently revised parts.
