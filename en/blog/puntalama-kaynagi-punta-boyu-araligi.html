Tack Welding: How Tack Length, Spacing and Sequence Affect Weld Quality

2026-07-01 6 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

Tack welds are short welds that hold parts in position before welding. They are often treated as “preparation”, yet most tacks remain inside the final weld and become part of it. A poorly made tack carries through into the final weld as lack of fusion, cracking or distortion.

What Tacks Do

  • Fix parts at the position and gap shown on the drawing.
  • Maintain root gap and joint geometry (see joint preparation).
  • Resist shrinkage during welding and limit distortion.
  • Allow the part to be removed from the fixture and welded on a positioner.

A Tack Is a Weld

For tacks that will be incorporated into the final weld, the rule is simple: the tack must meet the same conditions as the weld itself. That means:

  • made to a suitable WPS,
  • by a qualified welder or operator,
  • with preheat applied to the tack if preheat is required,
  • with the same or compatible consumable.

On thick or high-strength steels, short tacks made without preheat cool fast, harden and can crack. A short, small tack cools much faster than a long weld in the same material, so preheat for tacking is often at least as important as for the main weld (see preheat calculation).

Tack Length

EN 1090-2 sets a minimum length for tacks incorporated in the final weld: the lesser of four times the thickness of the thicker part or 50 mm. A shorter tack is accepted only if shown to be satisfactory by test. For example, on 10 mm plate a tack should be at least 40 mm, and on 20 mm plate at least 50 mm.

Tack Spacing

Spacing depends on material thickness, part length, welding process and distortion tendency. As a general approach, tacks are closer together on thin sheet and further apart on thick parts. Too few tacks let the gap close or open during welding; too many add work and defect risk. In series work, tack positions and number are standardised together with the fixture.

Tacking Sequence

  • On long joints, tack both ends first, then the middle, then the points in between, so the part does not creep in one direction.
  • On symmetrical parts, tack opposite sides alternately.
  • Fix critical dimensions (hole axes, connection points) first, then the rest.

Preparing Incorporated Tacks

  • Clean tacks of slag and spatter.
  • Remove tacks with cracks, porosity or lack of fusion before welding.
  • Where needed, grind tack ends to a taper so the final weld ties in smoothly.
  • Size tacks left in the root so they fuse completely with the root pass.

Bridge Tacks and Temporary Attachments

Bridging pieces or temporary lugs are sometimes welded outside the joint to hold the gap. These are welds too and need the same care. When removed, the parent metal must not be damaged, the remaining mark must be ground smooth and, where required, magnetic particle or penetrant testing confirms there are no cracks. On high-strength steels the location and removal of temporary attachments are kept under particular control.

Common Mistakes

  • Tacking a cold part on a job that requires preheat.
  • Welding over a tack without fusing it, leaving lack of fusion inside the weld.
  • Welding over a cracked tack.
  • Placing tacks randomly outside the joint on the parent metal, leaving arc strikes.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a tack weld be?

Under EN 1090-2, tacks incorporated in the final weld should be no shorter than the lesser of four times the thickness of the thicker part or 50 mm; shorter only if proven by test.

Do tack welds need preheat?

Yes, if the main weld requires preheat. Short tacks cool fast, so the risk of hardening and cracking is even higher.

What is the right tacking sequence?

On long joints, both ends first, then the middle and the points between; on symmetrical parts, opposite sides alternately.

What should be done with a cracked tack?

Remove it completely and re-tack before welding; welding over it carries the crack into the final weld.
