Welding Duplex Stainless Steel (2205): Ferrite Balance, Heat Input and Filler

2026-05-07 6 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

Duplex stainless steels have a two-phase structure with roughly equal amounts of ferrite and austenite. It gives about twice the yield strength of 316L and much better resistance to chloride stress corrosion cracking. They are increasingly used in chemical plants, seawater systems, tanks and food equipment. That advantage, however, depends on keeping the phase balance during welding.

The Real Issue: Phase Balance

If the weld zone cools too fast, there is not enough time for austenite to form from ferrite and the structure becomes overly ferritic; toughness and corrosion resistance drop. If it cools too slowly or stays long at 700–950 °C, intermetallic phases such as brittle sigma phase form and severely impair toughness and corrosion resistance. The aim is balanced cooling between the two.

Ferrite in weld metal and HAZ is generally required at roughly 30–70%; the exact range is set by the specification.

Heat Input and Interpass Temperature

  • 2205: heat input is usually kept at about 0.5–2.5 kJ/mm; interpass temperature mostly limited to 150 °C.
  • Super duplex 2507: a narrower window; heat input about 0.2–1.5 kJ/mm, interpass mostly limited to 100 °C.
  • Very thin root passes and very low heat input lead to excess ferrite; very heavy passes and hot working lead to intermetallics.
  • Preheat is normally not needed; light warming may be used only to prevent condensation.

Consumables

Duplex filler contains more nickel than the parent metal. The standard choice for 2205 is 2209 (22 9 3 N L to ISO 14343); the extra nickel ensures enough austenite forms in the fast-cooling weld metal. For super duplex, 2594 type (25 9 4 N L) wires are used. Autogenous (filler-free) welding causes an overly ferritic structure and is generally to be avoided.

Shielding and Backing Gas

Nitrogen promotes austenite. To limit nitrogen loss from the surface during welding, TIG shielding gases with a small addition of nitrogen (typically 1–2%) to argon and nitrogen-bearing backing gas can be used. Root-side oxidation is, as with standard stainless, where corrosion starts (see pickling and passivation).

Inspection and Testing

  • Ferrite measurement: ferrite content in weld metal, and in the HAZ where required, is measured with a magnetic instrument.
  • Procedure qualification: in addition to ISO 15614-1 tests, the specification may require metallography, impact testing and ASTM G48 pitting tests. ASTM A923 tests for detrimental intermetallic phases are also a common reference.
  • Heat treatment: no post-weld stress relief; if needed, only full solution annealing, which is impractical for most fabricated parts.

Joining to Carbon Steel

When a duplex part must be welded to carbon steel, dilution and phase balance are assessed together; 309L or duplex-type fillers are selected depending on conditions (see stainless to carbon steel welding).

Frequently Asked Questions

Which filler is used for duplex 2205?

Usually 2209 (ISO 14343 22 9 3 N L). The extra nickel ensures enough austenite forms in the fast-cooling weld metal.

What heat input is right for duplex?

Usually about 0.5–2.5 kJ/mm for 2205 and about 0.2–1.5 kJ/mm for super duplex 2507; the exact value is set in the WPS and specification.

What is sigma phase?

A brittle intermetallic phase that forms in duplex steels held at 700–950 °C; it reduces toughness and corrosion resistance.

How is ferrite content checked in duplex welds?

With a magnetic ferrite measuring instrument on the weld metal; roughly 30–70% is generally targeted.
