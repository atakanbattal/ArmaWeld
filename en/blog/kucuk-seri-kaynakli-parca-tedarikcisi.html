Finding a Supplier for Low-Volume, High-Mix Welded Parts

2026-01-08 7 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

Most machine builders do not buy thousands of the same part. The typical picture is hundreds of part numbers, each with demand from a few to a few hundred pieces a year. This "high mix, low volume" structure does not suit shops set up for large series; to them such jobs mean high set-up time and low efficiency. The right supplier is one that puts small batches at the centre of its business model.

What Drives Cost in Small Batches?

ItemLarge seriesSmall batch
Set-up and programmingSpread over thousands of partsA significant share per piece
FixtureDedicated, fast fixture is economicalModular fixture or tacking without a fixture
Drawing review and planningDone onceReviewed again with each order
MaterialBulk purchaseEffect of scrap and minimum order quantities
DocumentationOne report per batchThe same reporting load on few parts

Comparing small-batch unit prices with large-series prices is therefore misleading. The real question is how to reduce set-up and fixed costs (see prototype or series).

Ways to Reduce Cost

  • Batch consolidation: one order of forty instead of four orders of ten a year cuts set-up cost to a quarter; balance it against inventory cost.
  • Framework orders: with annual quantity and call-off logic known, the supplier can plan material and cutting in bulk (see framework orders).
  • Part families: giving similar parts to the same supplier shares similar set-ups and fixtures.
  • Standard material: common plate thicknesses and sections can be stocked; special grades and sizes extend lead time.
  • Clear drawings: a fully dimensioned drawing cuts the back-and-forth repeated with every order.

The Right Supplier Profile

  • A planning and tracking system that can manage many different work orders at once.
  • Broad WPS and welder certificate coverage; a new part should not need a new procedure.
  • Modular fixtures and a flexible shop layout.
  • Fast partners for cutting, bending and surface treatment.
  • A structure that treats small orders seriously, with a reasonable minimum order value.
  • Revision control, so different revisions of the same part never get mixed up.

Set the Documentation Load Right

Asking for a full data book for every part adds a lot of cost in small batches. Critical parts may need full documentation, while a material certificate and declaration of conformity can be enough for others. Classifying documentation level by part criticality reduces both cost and lead time (see data book and MDR).

Spare Parts and One-Offs

At the far end of small batches are single spare parts. Here speed often matters more than price; a stopped machine costs more than the part. Parts with a drawing and a production history are made much faster, so keeping drawings and WPS records for small-batch parts in order at the supplier pays off in the long run.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is the unit price high in small batches?

Fixed costs such as set-up, programming, fixtures, planning and documentation are spread over few parts.

How can small-batch cost be reduced?

By consolidating batches, framework orders, giving part families to one supplier, using standard material and clear drawings.

Does every small-batch part need a data book?

Usually not; documentation level should be set by part criticality.

What should a good small-batch supplier have?

The ability to manage many work orders, broad WPS coverage, modular fixtures, fast partners and good revision control.
