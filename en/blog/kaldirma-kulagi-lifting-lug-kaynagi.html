Lifting Lug Welding: A Small Part with a Big Responsibility

2026-03-23 6 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

Lifting lugs on a machine housing, frame, tank or container are often dealt with by a couple of dimensions on the drawing. Yet the entire weight of the part suddenly rests on these few welds, and the load usually arrives at an angle and with some shock. Lug failures are rare but their consequences are serious, so lugs deserve particular attention despite their size.

Design Considerations

  • Load and direction: the lug is sized for total weight, number of lugs, sling angle and dynamic effect. As the sling angle grows, the lug force increases and a side component appears.
  • Geometry: the hole diameter must suit the shackle pin, and the material around the hole must be sufficient against tear-out and bearing.
  • Orientation: the lug plane must be aligned with the direction of load. A lug loaded sideways can fail well below its design capacity.
  • Reinforcement: if the lug is welded to a thin plate or section, a doubler plate or internal stiffener is needed to spread the load.
  • Centre of gravity: lug positions are chosen relative to the centre of gravity; otherwise the part tilts or rotates when lifted.

Welding Requirements

Lug connections are usually designed as full-penetration butt or K-welds with a high quality level (see ISO 5817 quality levels). If fillet welds are used, the throat size must be verified by calculation (see weld strength calculation). On thick plates, preheat and hydrogen control matter (see hydrogen and cold cracking).

Risk of Lamellar Tearing

When a lug is welded as a T onto a plate surface, the load acts through the plate thickness. In thick plates this can lead to lamellar tearing. For critical applications, steel with guaranteed through-thickness properties (Z quality), a suitable joint detail or a through-plate lug is preferred (see lamellar tearing and Z quality).

Inspection

  • Visual inspection of all lug welds.
  • MT on fillet and T-joints; UT on full-penetration welds.
  • For thick plates, UT lamination check of the plate before welding may be required.
  • Dimensional report on hole size and lug position.

NDT must be performed and reported by personnel at the right level (see ISO 9712 personnel levels).

Common Mistakes

  • Attaching the lug with a fillet weld on one side only.
  • Drilling the hole before welding so that it goes oval afterwards.
  • Hole diameter shrinking after painting or galvanising.
  • No capacity marking on the lugs.
  • Not stating on the drawing that lugs must be cut off and ground after transport, if that is intended.

Frequently Asked Questions

How should a lifting lug be welded?

Usually with a full-penetration butt or K-weld; if fillet welds are used, the throat size must be verified by calculation.

Which NDT is applied to lifting lugs?

Visual inspection, MT on fillet and T-joints, UT on full-penetration welds.

Why does lamellar tearing matter for lugs?

When a lug is welded onto a plate surface, the load acts through the thickness and can tear thick plates.

Can a lug be loaded sideways?

Not unless it was designed for it; side loading greatly reduces capacity.
