Dual Sourcing for Welded Parts: When It Makes Sense and How to Set It Up

2025-11-26 7 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

Buying a welded part from one workshop is the cheapest route while things go well. But when that workshop has a fire, a long machine breakdown, loses a key welder or runs out of capacity, the customer's assembly line stops too. A dual sourcing strategy reduces that risk at an acceptable cost.

Hidden Risks of a Single Supplier

  • Capacity risk: when the supplier focuses on a big order, your part may be put on hold.
  • Operational risk: breakdowns, fire, flooding or a long power outage.
  • Knowledge risk: if only a few people really know the part, their departure affects quality.
  • Negotiating power: a buyer with no alternative is weak in price talks.
  • Regional risk: logistics disruption, border crossings or regional crises.

Which Parts Need a Second Source?

A second supplier for every part is unnecessary cost. A simple assessment helps to prioritise:

CriterionPoints towards dual sourcing
Impact on line stopWithout the part, the end product cannot ship
Annual volumeContinuous and predictable demand
Technical difficultyMedium; very special processes make a second source harder
StockabilityBulky parts that cannot be stocked increase risk
Current supplier statusAt capacity limit or erratic performance

For low-volume parts that are easy to stock, a single supplier plus safety stock is usually more economical.

Volume Split Models

  • Share split: for example 70% main supplier, 30% second supplier. Both keep the process warm.
  • Split by part family: similar parts go to different suppliers, and each is the approved backup for the other's family.
  • Dormant backup: the second supplier is approved and runs a few small batches a year. Low cost, but a longer ramp-up.
  • Regional split: two suppliers in different countries or regions, spreading logistics and geopolitical risk (see nearshoring and Turkey).

Getting the Same Quality from Two Suppliers

The biggest danger with dual sourcing is "same drawing, different part" from two workshops. To prevent it:

  • State the weld class, quality level (e.g. ISO 5817 B or C) and general tolerances clearly on the drawing (see ISO 13920 tolerance table).
  • Use the same inspection plan and NDT rates for both suppliers.
  • Define a common check gauge or the same measuring method for critical dimensions.
  • Keep one clear surface treatment specification.
  • Put both suppliers through first article approval and test their parts mixed in assembly.

Cost Impact

A second supplier brings extra cost: a second fixture set, another first article approval, two supplier audits and a slightly higher unit price because of smaller batches. Compare this with the cost of a line stop. Many buyers decide quickly once they see that one day of downtime exceeds the annual cost of dual sourcing.

Choosing the Second Supplier

  • It should not share the main supplier's risks; the same region, energy source or steel supplier concentrates risk.
  • The required welding procedures and certificates should already be in place (see choosing a contract welding supplier).
  • It must be willing to work with small batches; the "backup" role does not suit every workshop.
  • Agree minimum annual volume and call-off logic in a framework agreement (see framework orders).

Frequently Asked Questions

What is dual sourcing?

Being able to buy the same part from two approved suppliers, to reduce the risk of supply interruption.

Does every part need a second supplier?

No. Parts with high line-stop impact, continuous demand and poor stockability come first.

How do I make sure parts from two suppliers are identical?

With clear quality levels, a common inspection plan, the same measuring method and first article approval for both.

Does dual sourcing increase cost?

There are extra costs for fixtures, approvals and audits; compare them with the cost of a possible line stop.
