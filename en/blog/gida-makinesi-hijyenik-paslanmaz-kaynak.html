Hygienic Stainless Welding for Food Machinery: Design and Fabrication Rules

2026-11-09 8 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

On food machinery a weld is not just about strength. A pore, a gap or a rough bead can become a spot where product residue and bacteria cling and cleaning cannot reach. Hygienic design rules therefore directly dictate how welded fabrication is done.

Zones

A machine is considered in three zones:

  • Food zone: surfaces in direct contact with product; the strictest rules apply.
  • Splash zone: surfaces product may splash onto but does not return from.
  • Non-food zone: frame, guards and drives; still required to be cleanable.

Weld quality, surface finish and detailing differ by zone; this split should be clear at the quotation and drawing stage.

Material Choice

  • 1.4301 / 1.4307 (304 / 304L): common for general food machinery.
  • 1.4404 (316L): better for salty or acidic products and aggressive cleaning chemicals.
  • Low-carbon (L) grades: reduce intergranular corrosion risk at the weld.
  • Sulphur-bearing free-machining grades (1.4305): lower corrosion resistance, so not preferred for product contact.

Detail Design

  • Internal corners: radiused transitions instead of sharp corners (generally at least 3 mm in the EHEDG approach).
  • Self-draining: horizontal surfaces slightly sloped so water and product do not collect.
  • Hollow sections: fully closed and sealed by welding; no open-ended tubes or boxes.
  • Dead zones: pockets and blind spots where product stagnates are designed out.
  • Fasteners: exposed threads and bolt heads avoided in the product zone where possible.
  • Frame: fewer horizontal surfaces near the floor; feet leave enough clearance for cleaning.

Surface Roughness

A common target for product contact surfaces is Ra ≤ 0.8 µm. Weld beads are ground and polished to this level. Surface structure matters as much as the number: deep scratches, folded grinding marks and embedded particles impair cleanability.

Post-Weld Treatment

  • Heat tint removal: heat tint around the weld lowers corrosion resistance; it is removed by pickling or mechanical methods.
  • Passivation: supports re-formation of the protective chromium oxide layer.
  • Iron contamination: discs, brushes and tools shared with carbon steel transfer iron to stainless; separate tools are a must.

Inspection and Documentation

  • Visual testing (VT) of welds and penetrant testing (PT) where needed,
  • Root-side inspection (directly if accessible, otherwise by endoscope),
  • Surface roughness measurements,
  • Material certificates and records needed for food contact compliance,
  • Welding procedures (WPS) and welder qualifications used.

Hygienic design compliance of the machine as a whole rests with the machine manufacturer. The fabricator supplies parts and records that meet the rules given.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which stainless is used for food machinery?

1.4301/1.4307 (304/304L) for general use; 1.4404 (316L) for salty or acidic products and aggressive cleaning.

What roughness should product contact surfaces have?

A common target is Ra ≤ 0.8 µm; weld beads are ground and polished to reach it.

Why are intermittent welds avoided on food machinery?

Gaps between welds form crevices that cannot be cleaned and can harbour bacteria.

Why must heat tint be removed?

Corrosion resistance drops in the heat-tinted zone; pickling and passivation restore protection.
