Pulsed MIG/MAG Welding: Transfer Modes and When a Pulsed Arc Makes a Difference

2026-06-08 6 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

In MIG/MAG welding the metal melting off the wire tip crosses into the pool in different ways. This is called the transfer mode, and it directly determines bead quality, spatter, penetration and heat input. The pulsed arc is the most controlled of these modes, but it is not needed for every job.

The Four Basic Transfer Modes

ModeCurrent/voltageCharacteristicsTypical use
Short circuitLowWire touches the pool and short-circuits; low heat inputThin sheet, root runs, positional welding
GlobularMediumLarge, irregular droplets; high spatterA transition range generally avoided
SprayHighFine droplets, spatter-free and deep penetration; high heat inputThick parts, flat position
PulsedAlternates between peak and backgroundOne droplet per pulse; near-spray quality at lower average heatStainless, aluminium, positional welding, visible welds

How a Pulsed Arc Works

The power source switches the current tens to hundreds of times per second between a high peak current and a low background current. At peak the droplet at the wire tip is detached; at background the arc stays alight but the wire tip only heats. Ideally one droplet transfers per pulse. This gives the spatter-free, regular transfer of a spray arc at a much lower average current.

Modern machines handle these settings with synergic programs: the user selects material, wire diameter and gas, and when the wire feed speed changes, the machine adjusts the pulse parameters automatically.

Advantages of a Pulsed Arc

  • Little spatter: less grinding and cleaning, a major advantage on visible welds.
  • Lower heat input: less distortion on thin and medium thicknesses; on stainless, lower risk of heat tint and carbide precipitation.
  • Positional welding: near-spray quality in vertical and overhead positions, with better fusion than short circuit.
  • Aluminium: a wider working range and a stable arc. Double pulse can produce a TIG-like rippled appearance.
  • Sidewall fusion: a safer option against the lack-of-fusion (cold lap) risk seen with short-circuit transfer.

Limitations

  • Gas: pulsed and spray arcs need argon-rich gas (for carbon steel e.g. M20 or M21 groups, for aluminium pure argon). True spray or pulse cannot be achieved with 100% CO₂ (see shielding gas selection).
  • Equipment: a power source with pulse capability and synergic programs is required.
  • Thick parts: in the flat position on heavy sections, conventional spray or submerged arc can give higher deposition.
  • Parameter discipline: if program, wire and gas do not match, the benefits are lost.

Heat Input: A Pulsed-Arc Caveat

The classic formula calculates heat input from average voltage and average current (see heat input). With waveform-controlled arcs such as pulse, current and voltage change constantly, so the product of averages may not reflect the true power. ISO 15614-1 and ISO/TR 18491 therefore provide for determining heat input by instantaneous power or instantaneous energy measurement for waveform-controlled processes. Modern machines can display this value directly. On jobs with heat input limits (for example high-strength steels or joints with impact requirements), the WPS should state clearly which method the value is based on.

What Goes in the WPS?

  • Transfer mode (short circuit, spray or pulse) and, where relevant, the synergic program.
  • Wire diameter, wire feed speed range and shielding gas.
  • Heat input range and calculation method.
  • Position, travel speed and torch angle.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is pulsed welding?

MIG/MAG welding in which the current switches rapidly between a high peak and a low background value. One droplet detaches per pulse, giving a near-spray, low-spatter weld at lower average heat.

When is pulsed welding used?

It helps most on stainless and aluminium, thin and medium thicknesses, positional welding and visible welds.

Can pulsed welding be done with 100% CO₂?

No. Pulsed and spray arcs need argon-rich gas; a true pulsed arc cannot be achieved with 100% CO₂.

How is heat input calculated with a pulsed arc?

For waveform-controlled arcs, ISO 15614-1 and ISO/TR 18491 recommend calculating it from instantaneous power or instantaneous energy measurement.
