Fillet Weld Throat Thickness: a vs z, Sizing and Tolerances

2026-01-29 6 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

Most welds on a fabricated part are fillet welds. A small value next to the symbol on the drawing, such as a5, decides both the load the joint carries and the welding time and cost. This article explains what the a dimension is, how it differs from z, how it is chosen and how it is checked in the shop.

a Dimension and z Dimension

  • a (throat thickness): the height, from the root to the hypotenuse, of the largest isosceles triangle that fits in the weld cross-section. Strength calculations use this value.
  • z (leg length): the length of the weld edge on the part surface. This is what the shop sees and measures directly.

For an equal-leg fillet weld on a 90° joint: a ≈ 0.7 × z, so z ≈ 1.4 × a.

How It Appears on the Drawing

Under ISO 2553 the size is written to the left of the fillet symbol. "a5" means throat thickness, "z7" means leg length. If no letter is given, many European drawings imply the a dimension, but that is an assumption and should be clarified at quotation. Weld length, and pitch for intermittent welds, go to the right, e.g. a4 3×50 (150). See our weld symbols guide for more.

How Is a Chosen?

  1. Load calculation: for structural joints, a is calculated to design codes such as EN 1993-1-8. In the simplified method, the force a weld can carry per unit length is proportional to a.
  2. Lower limit: EN 1993-1-8 requires load-bearing fillet welds to have a throat of at least 3 mm.
  3. Match to plate thickness: as a practical rule, a should not exceed about 0.7 times the thinner part. A larger weld usually adds no capacity, only heat input and distortion.
  4. Single-run limit: with MAG, around a5-a6 can be achieved in one run. Larger sizes need multi-run welding and time rises quickly.

Why Bigger Welds Cost More

Weld metal volume in a fillet weld rises with the square of a. Going from a5 to a7 roughly doubles the weld metal (49/25 ≈ 1.96), plus extra runs, more heat input and more time spent correcting distortion. A weld enlarged "just in case" adds cost without making the part stronger.

Deep Penetration

High-penetration processes such as submerged arc welding fuse beyond the root. EN 1993-1-8 allows this extra penetration to be counted only if it is demonstrated by a procedure test. A weld that looks smaller on the surface can then provide the same calculated capacity.

ISO 5817 Tolerances on a

ISO 5817 limits both insufficient and excessive throat thickness on fillet welds:

How It Is Measured

The a dimension is measured during visual inspection with a fillet weld gauge or multi-purpose weld gauge resting on both part surfaces. On a convex weld the reading gives the real throat; on a concave weld the surface profile governs, so measurement needs more care. For critical joints, a macro section can be taken from a sample.

Pre-Quotation Checklist

  1. Does the drawing clearly state a or z?
  2. Is a consistent with the thinner part's thickness?
  3. Would an intermittent weld do, or is a continuous weld really needed?
  4. Is the required quality level (B, C, D) stated?

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the leg length of an a5 fillet weld?

For an equal-leg fillet weld on a 90° joint, z ≈ 1.4 × a, so an a5 weld has a leg length of about 7 mm.

What is the difference between a and z?

a is the throat thickness in the weld cross-section, used in calculations. z is the leg length on the part surface, seen directly in the shop.

What is the minimum fillet weld throat?

EN 1993-1-8 requires load-bearing fillet welds to have a throat of at least 3 mm. For non-structural seams, the customer specification governs.

Does a larger weld always make the part stronger?

No. Beyond a certain point the plate becomes the weak link; a bigger weld only adds heat input, distortion and cost.
