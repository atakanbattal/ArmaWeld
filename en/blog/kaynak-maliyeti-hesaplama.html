Welding Cost Calculation: Wire Consumption, Arc Time and Cost per Metre

2026-02-23 7 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

In the price of a welded part, welding labour is often the largest item. It can be calculated rather than guessed, using a few simple quantities: the weld metal to be deposited, the deposition rate of the process and how long the welder actually keeps the arc burning. This article walks through the steps with a worked example.

1. Weld Metal Weight

Start with the cross-sectional area of the weld. For an equal-leg fillet weld the theoretical area is A ≈ a² (mm²). For butt welds the area follows from groove geometry (see our joint preparation guide). Usually 10-20% is added for reinforcement.

Weld metal per metre (g/m) ≈ A (mm²) × 7.85 × (1 + reinforcement allowance)

2. Consumable Quantity

Not all purchased wire ends up as weld metal; some is lost as spatter, slag or stub ends. Deposition efficiency depends on the process:

  • MAG with solid wire: about 90-95%
  • Flux-cored wire (FCAW): about 80-90%
  • Covered electrodes (MMA): about 55-65% (stub and slag losses)
  • Submerged arc (SAW): above 95% for the wire; flux is calculated separately

Wire required (kg/m) = weld metal (kg/m) / deposition efficiency

3. Arc Time

Deposition rate is the weld metal a process deposits per hour. With 1.2 mm solid wire MAG in the flat position it is typically 3-5 kg/h, clearly lower in vertical position. Single-wire SAW can reach 6-10 kg/h and more.

Arc time (min/m) = weld metal (kg/m) / deposition rate (kg/h) × 60

4. Operator Factor

Not all of a welder’s working time is arc time. Turning parts, tacking, slag removal, torch cleaning and measuring also take time. The ratio of arc-on time to total time is the operator factor. In manual welding it is often 20-40%; in mechanized welding and well-fixtured work it is higher.

Welding labour time (min/m) = arc time / operator factor

5. Gas and Energy

  • Shielding gas: flow rate (l/min) × arc time. At 15 l/min, every arc minute uses 15 litres.
  • Energy: current × voltage × arc time / machine efficiency. Usually a small share of the total.

Worked Example: a5 Fillet Weld, MAG, PB Position

  1. Weld metal: ≈ 0.226 kg/m
  2. Wire: 0.226 / 0.93 ≈ 0.243 kg/m
  3. Arc time: 0.226 / 4 kg/h × 60 ≈ 3.4 min/m
  4. With a 30% operator factor, labour time: 3.4 / 0.30 ≈ 11.3 min/m
  5. Gas: 15 l/min × 3.4 min ≈ 51 l/m

Cost per metre = labour time × hourly rate + wire × wire price + gas × gas price. Labour is usually several times the consumables. Had the same weld been specified as a6, weld metal would rise by 44% and time by a similar proportion.

What the Calculation Leaves Out

On a real part, non-welding time often exceeds arc time: material preparation, fit-up and tacking, fixture set-up, straightening, grinding, cleaning, inspection and documentation. In a quotation these are handled as separate items (see what drives welded fabrication prices).

Decisions That Reduce Cost

  • Choose weld size by calculation, not "just in case".
  • Use X or U grooves instead of V on thick plate.
  • Design the part so it can be welded in flat and horizontal positions.
  • Shorten repetitive fit-up with fixtures.
  • Move long straight seams to high-deposition processes such as SAW.
  • Remove unnecessary grinding and cosmetic requirements.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is wire consumption per metre calculated?

Weld cross-section (mm²) × 7.85 gives weld metal in grams per metre; add reinforcement and divide by the deposition efficiency of the process.

How much wire does an a5 fillet weld use per metre?

About 0.23 kg of weld metal; with solid-wire MAG efficiency this means roughly 0.24-0.25 kg of wire.

What is the operator factor?

The ratio of arc-on time to the welder’s total working time. In manual welding it is often 20-40%.

What is the largest welding cost item?

In most welded fabrication it is labour time. Consumables and gas are usually a small fraction of labour.
