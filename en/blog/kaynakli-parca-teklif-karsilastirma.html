Comparing Quotes for Welded Parts: Look Beyond the Unit Price

2025-12-08 7 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

You received quotes from three suppliers for the same drawing and the prices differ by 40%. The first reflex is to pick the cheapest. With welded parts, however, most of the price gap comes from quotes not covering the same thing. A proper comparison first equalises scope, then looks at total cost.

Where Do Price Differences Come From?

  • Material: one may have priced S355JR, another S355J2; one may include a 3.1 certificate, the other not.
  • Inspection: visual inspection and 10% UT differ considerably in cost.
  • Surface: primer only, a full paint system or galvanising?
  • Documentation: are dimensional reports, NDT reports and a data book included?
  • Delivery terms: EXW and DAP differ in freight, packaging and customs (see export packaging and Incoterms).
  • One-off costs: are fixtures, programming and first article approval listed separately or buried in the unit price?

Step 2: Calculate Total Cost

What the part really costs you is more than the unit price:

  • Unit price × annual quantity
  • + One-off costs (fixtures, first article, programming)
  • + Freight, packaging, customs and insurance
  • + Incoming inspection and the cost of possible rejections
  • + Inventory cost (a longer lead time means more stock)
  • + The financing effect of payment terms

A distant supplier's low unit price can be cancelled out by high freight and a larger safety stock. Conversely, a slightly more expensive supplier with complete documentation can cut the time spent on incoming inspection.

Step 3: Score the Risks

For factors other than price, a simple scoring table works: how well the technical questions were asked, how realistic the lead time is, quality system and certificates, references, speed of communication. A supplier who asks no questions at quotation stage may not have really studied the drawing.

Suspiciously Low Quotes

When one quote is far below the others, ask:

  • What date is the material price based on, and how long is it valid?
  • How was the welding time estimated? (see welding cost calculation)
  • Are NDT and documentation really included?
  • Is there capacity, or will it be planned after the order?

A low price is not always bad; an efficient process or suitable material in stock can be a real advantage. What matters is understanding the reason for the gap.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do welded part quotes differ so much in price?

Usually the quotes do not cover the same scope: material certificates, NDT, surface treatment, documentation, freight and one-off costs are handled differently.

How do I compare quotes fairly?

First equalise scope in one table, get prices for missing items, then calculate total cost.

What goes into total cost?

Unit price, one-off costs, freight and customs, incoming inspection, inventory cost and the financing effect of payment terms.

Is choosing the lowest quote risky?

It is risky without understanding the reason for the gap; ask about material, NDT and capacity assumptions.
