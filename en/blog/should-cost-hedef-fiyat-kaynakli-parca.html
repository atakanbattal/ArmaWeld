Should-Cost and Target Price for Welded Parts: What Should a Part Cost?

2025-12-15 8 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

A should-cost analysis estimates not what a part "sells for" but what it "should cost" at an efficient manufacturer. Purchasing teams use it to base supplier discussions on numbers, to spot abnormal quotes and to find cost reduction opportunities in the design. For welded parts the method is quite transparent, because most of the cost comes from measurable items.

Estimating Welding Time

Welding is usually the most debated item. A practical approach: calculate the weld metal weight from the weld cross-section, divide by the process deposition rate, then divide the resulting arc time by an arc time ratio (the share of total working time actually spent welding). For manual MAG welding this ratio is usually low; turning the part, cleaning, changing position and in-process checks take a large share of the time. See welding cost calculation for details.

What Does the Shop Rate Cover?

A shop hourly rate is not just the welder's wage. Equipment depreciation, consumables other than gas and wire, energy, buildings, quality control, planning and management are all spread over it. A should-cost model uses a reasonable rate range for the country and region; the goal is not to know the supplier's internal cost exactly but to get the order of magnitude right.

How to Build a Target Price

  1. Find the should-cost total.
  2. Add a reasonable profit margin; assuming a margin near zero does not build a sustainable supply relationship.
  3. Adjust for volume, batch size and delivery terms.
  4. Compare with incoming quotes and discuss large deviations item by item (see comparing quotes).

Cost Discussions with Suppliers

Should-cost works best as a common language rather than a negotiating weapon. Instead of "your price is too high", asking "you show 3 hours of welding, our model shows 2; where does the difference come from?" brings out the right information and opens the door to design improvements. Often the gap comes from an oversized fillet weld, an unnecessarily tight tolerance or an expensive surface requirement on the drawing (see cost optimisation).

Common Calculation Mistakes

  • Using net weight and ignoring scrap and cutting losses.
  • Calculating arc time only and forgetting preparation and handling.
  • Ignoring the per-piece effect of set-up time in small batches.
  • Counting NDT, dimensional reports and documentation as zero.
  • Not checking the date and validity of the material price.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a should-cost analysis?

A method of estimating what a part should cost at an efficient manufacturer from material, labour, process and overhead items.

How is welding cost estimated?

Weld metal weight is derived from the cross-section, divided by deposition rate to get arc time, and converted to total time with an arc time ratio.

Is the target price the same as should-cost?

No. The target price adds a reasonable profit and adjustments for volume, batch size and delivery terms to the should-cost total.

What is the most common mistake?

Leaving out scrap and preparation time, and counting NDT and documentation as zero.
