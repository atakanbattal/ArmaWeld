Hydrogen Control in Welding: How Cold Cracking Forms and How to Prevent It

2026-06-19 7 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

Cold cracking is a defect that can appear hours or even days after welding, often growing in the HAZ or weld metal without breaking the surface. A part that passed visual inspection can show cracks in an ultrasonic test the next day. The main culprit is hydrogen picked up during welding.

Four Conditions for Cold Cracking

Hydrogen-induced cracking occurs when four conditions coincide:

  1. Hydrogen: diffusible hydrogen entering the weld metal.
  2. Susceptible microstructure: a hard structure formed on fast cooling (high carbon equivalent, thick section, low heat input).
  3. Stress: residual stresses from weld shrinkage and restraint.
  4. Low temperature: typically below about 200 °C, where hydrogen migrates, accumulates and forms cracks.

Removing any one of these prevents cracking. In practice the most effective routes are reducing hydrogen and slowing cooling with preheat.

Where Does Hydrogen Come From?

  • Moisture absorbed by electrode coatings or submerged arc flux.
  • Oil, dirt or rust on the wire surface.
  • Oil, paint, primer, rust and condensation on the parent metal.
  • Humid surroundings and dew on cold parts.
  • Moisture-laden flux fill in cored wires.

Hydrogen Classes

Consumables are classified by diffusible hydrogen measured to ISO 3690:

ClassDiffusible hydrogen (ml/100 g weld metal)Assessment
H5≤ 5Very low; preferred for high-strength and thick steels
H10≤ 10Low; common for general structural steel
H15≤ 15Moderate; usually not sufficient for critical work

EN 1011-2 classifies hydrogen level with letters A–E in the preheat calculation; lower hydrogen means lower preheat for the same steel and thickness (see preheat calculation).

Practical Measures

  • Consumable selection: for critical work, H5 basic electrodes, low-hydrogen cored wire or solid wire (see types of electrodes).
  • Baking and storage: basic electrodes and submerged arc fluxes are baked to the manufacturer’s instructions and kept in heated cabinets or quivers before use; exposure time is limited.
  • Surface cleanliness: the joint and surrounding area are free of oil, paint, rust and moisture.
  • Preheat and interpass temperature: slow cooling, reduce hard microstructure and give hydrogen time to diffuse out.
  • Dehydrogenation (post-heating): holding the part at a set temperature (typically about 200–300 °C) for a few hours straight after welding, without letting it cool, lets hydrogen escape. Temperature and time follow the specification or procedure qualification.
  • Welding sequence: sequences that increase restraint stress are avoided.

Hold Time Before Inspection

Because cold cracking can be delayed, final inspection must take place not immediately after welding but after a set time. EN 1090-2 defines minimum hold times before NDT depending on steel grade, thickness and heat input; as thickness and strength rise, the time grows from cooling only to several hours and up to 40 hours. ISO 17635 likewise expects the timing of inspection to be set in the application standard. If the hold time is skipped, a part that passed inspection can crack later.

Highest-Risk Work

  • S460 and above, especially quenched and tempered steels (see welding S960QL).
  • Thick sections and highly restrained joints.
  • Welding in cold and humid conditions.
  • Repair welds, where restraint from the existing structure is high (see weld defects and repair).

Frequently Asked Questions

What is cold cracking?

Cracking that can form hours or days after the weld has cooled, caused by hydrogen, hard microstructure and stress together. It is also called delayed or hydrogen cracking.

What does an H5 electrode mean?

A very low-hydrogen consumable leaving at most 5 ml/100 g diffusible hydrogen in the weld metal.

How long after welding should NDT be done?

EN 1090-2 defines minimum hold times by steel grade, thickness and heat input; for thick, high-strength work this can be up to 40 hours.

What is a dehydrogenation heat treatment?

Holding the part, without letting it cool after welding, at typically about 200–300 °C for a few hours so hydrogen diffuses out.
