Weldment Instead of Casting: When It Makes Sense and How to Convert

2026-01-20 7 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

Machine housings, bearing boxes, press arms and large brackets have traditionally been cast. But at low volumes, in large sizes or for products whose design changes often, pattern costs and long foundry lead times become a burden. A structure built by welding steel plates, a weldment, is a strong alternative here. Not every casting should be converted, though; the decision rests on a few criteria.

Comparison

CriterionCastingWeldment
Up-front costPattern and tooling requiredNo tooling, a simple fixture is enough
Suitable volumeMedium and high seriesOne-off to medium series
Design changesRequire pattern changesApplied quickly through a drawing revision
WeightWall thickness can only be reduced so farSections optimised for load, usually lighter
StiffnessHigh for cast steel, lower modulus for grey ironHigh modulus of steel
Vibration dampingGrey iron (flake graphite) is excellentLower; can be improved with ribs and fill
Complex internal cavitiesEasy with coresDifficult or must be split into parts

When Does Switching Make Sense?

  • When annual volume is low and pattern cost weighs heavily per part.
  • When the part is large and a one-piece casting is difficult or expensive.
  • When the design is still evolving and frequent revisions are expected.
  • When casting supply has lead-time or quality problems (porosity, voids, dimensional deviation).
  • When there is a weight-reduction target.

By contrast, precision machine-tool beds that need high damping, pump housings with complex internal passages, or very high-volume small parts usually belong in casting.

Do Not Copy, Redesign

The most common mistake is trying to imitate the casting geometry one-to-one in plate. Castings have curved surfaces, varying wall thickness and rounded transitions; copying them with welds is expensive and unnecessary. The right approach is to understand the part's function and build the load path with flat plates, tubes, sections and ribs. Interface faces, bearing bores and mounting dimensions are kept; the form in between is redesigned freely.

Technical Points

  • Machining allowance: bearing and mating faces are machined after welding. Enough allowance and reference faces must be defined in the design from the start (see machining after welding).
  • Stress relief: in housings that will be precision machined, residual welding stresses can cause distortion after machining. Stress-relief annealing is planned with partners (see post-weld heat treatment).
  • Fatigue: in parts under cyclic load, the position and detail of welds matter; keep welds away from high-stress areas (see fatigue).
  • Damping: if vibration is critical, consider internal ribs, closed box sections or local fill material.

Conversion Steps

  1. Define the cast part's function, loads and critical dimensions.
  2. Prepare the welded design and verify it by calculation if needed.
  3. Build and machine a prototype, try it in assembly and in the field.
  4. Compare cost, weight and lead time with the casting.
  5. After approval, move to series production and put the drawing under revision control.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a casting be converted into a weldment?

Most machine housings, brackets and arms can; parts with complex internal passages or high damping needs usually stay cast.

Is a weldment cheaper than a casting?

At low and medium volumes, for large parts and changing designs it is usually more economical because there is no tooling cost.

Is a welded housing lighter than a casting?

Usually yes; sections are chosen for the load, so the same stiffness can be reached with less material.

How is distortion avoided in a welded housing?

With the right weld sequence, fixturing, stress relief where needed and sufficient machining allowance.
