Welding Corten Steel: S355J2W, the Right Filler and Design for Patina

2026-11-16 6 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

Weathering steels, widely known as Corten, form a protective rust layer, the patina, when used unpainted. They are chosen for architectural facades, bridges, landscape elements and container bodies. Uniform patina, however, depends on both the right welding consumable and the right design.

Standard and Grades

These steels are covered by EN 10025-5. “Corten” is a trade name; the standard designations are:

EN 10025-5 gradeImpact requirementNote
S235J0W / J2W27 J at 0 °C / −20 °CLower strength
S355J0WP27 J at 0 °CPhosphorus-enhanced, usually thin sheet; often called “Corten A”
S355J0W27 J at 0 °CGeneral structures
S355J2W27 J at −20 °CMost common; often referred to as the “Corten B” equivalent
S355K2W40 J at −20 °CThick and critical parts

Weathering resistance comes from alloying elements such as copper, chromium, nickel and phosphorus.

How Patina Forms

Patina forms through wet and dry cycles and matures within a few years in normal outdoor conditions. On surfaces that stay permanently wet, are exposed to salty marine air, heavy sulphur dioxide industrial atmospheres or are buried, no protective patina forms and the steel corrodes at a normal rate. Corten is therefore not the right choice for every environment (see ISO 12944 corrosivity categories).

Welding Consumables

The wire is the most critical decision. A weld made with standard S355 wire does not form the same patina as the parent metal; over the years it takes a different colour and corrodes faster. For visible, unpainted welds, Ni-Cu weathering wires that give corrosion resistance similar to the parent metal are used.

  • Single-pass visible welds: entirely with weathering wire.
  • Multi-pass thick welds: root and fill can be standard wire; at least two capping passes on the surface are made with weathering wire so the exposed weld metal reaches the right composition.
  • Parts to be painted: standard wire is usually sufficient; the decision follows the specification.

Welding Practice

Weldability is similar to S355. Preheat depends on carbon equivalent, thickness and hydrogen level (see preheat calculation). Rust, oil and mill scale must be removed before welding. After welding, slag and spatter should be removed; otherwise the patina forms differently at those spots.

Designing for Patina

  • No standing water: drain holes in horizontal pockets and tops of closed sections; avoid water-trapping details.
  • No narrow gaps: moisture in lap joints never dries; seal welds or sufficient clearance are needed.
  • Run-off staining: rust water during patina formation stains concrete and stone; drip edges and drainage must be planned.
  • Corrosion allowance: some material is lost while patina forms; leaving an allowance in the section for the design life is common practice.
  • Fasteners: bolts and nuts should also be of weathering material; galvanized bolts can cause problems through dissimilar metal contact.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which wire is used to weld Corten?

Ni-Cu weathering wire for visible, unpainted welds. In multi-pass welds, root and fill may be standard wire with at least two capping passes in weathering wire.

What are Corten A and Corten B?

Trade names. Corten A is usually associated with S355J0WP and Corten B with S355J2W (EN 10025-5).

Can Corten be used in any environment?

No. Permanently wet, salty marine, heavy sulphur industrial or buried conditions prevent a protective patina from forming.

Does Corten need preheating?

Weldability is similar to S355; preheat depends on thickness, carbon equivalent and hydrogen level.
