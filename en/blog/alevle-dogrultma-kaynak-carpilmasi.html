Flame Straightening: Rules for Correcting Weld Distortion with Heat

2026-06-25 6 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

Even the best-planned welding sequence does not eliminate distortion completely. For a beam, chassis or plate that is out of tolerance, one of the most common correction methods is flame straightening. Done right it is fast and effective; done wrong it can damage the material’s mechanical properties. That is why flame straightening is a controlled process as much as a manual skill.

How It Works

A small area of steel is heated rapidly. The heated area wants to expand but the cold surrounding material prevents it, so the area is permanently “upset” (shortened and thickened). As it cools it contracts and bends the part towards the heated side. Flame straightening uses the same mechanism that causes weld distortion, in the opposite direction and under control (see weld distortion).

Heating Patterns

PatternWhere it is usedEffect
Spot heatingBulging and buckling in thin sheetTightens the surface through local shrinkage
Line heatingAngular distortion of plates, flange rotationAngular correction along the heated line
Wedge (triangle) heatingCamber and sweep in beams and sectionsBends towards the wedge base

In wedge heating the apex of the triangle sits at the neutral axis and the base at the edge to be corrected; heating progresses from apex to base. Web and flange are heated together to correct the whole section.

Temperature Limits

The most important rule in flame straightening is not to exceed the maximum temperature. The limit depends on how the steel was produced:

  • As-rolled and normalised steels (e.g. S235, S355 +AR/+N): an upper limit of about 650 °C is generally applied; some sources accept slightly higher for short periods.
  • Thermomechanically rolled steels (M, ML): lower limits apply; high temperature can destroy the fine-grained structure and strength gained in rolling.
  • Quenched and tempered steels (Q, QL): must stay below the tempering temperature, otherwise strength is lost.

Exact values come from the steel producer’s recommendation and the specification (see S355 grades). EN 1090-2 requires the procedure used for flame correction, the maximum temperature and the cooling method to be defined and documented.

How Is Temperature Checked?

Judging temperature by the colour of the steel carries a large error depending on ambient light. Temperature-indicating crayons, contact thermometers or infrared measurement are used instead. With infrared, the surface emissivity setting matters.

Cooling

For structural steels the part is generally left to cool in air. Rapid water cooling can create hard, brittle zones in some steels, so it is not used unless the specification allows it, and it is particularly avoided on high-strength steels.

Stainless and Aluminium

  • Austenitic stainless: high thermal expansion and low conductivity make it respond strongly. But dwelling in roughly 450–850 °C can cause carbide precipitation and reduced corrosion resistance; low-carbon (L) grades are preferred and heating is kept short. Heat tint must be removed afterwards.
  • Aluminium: low melting point and no colour change, so temperature cannot be judged by eye. On heat-treatable alloys straightening can cause loss of strength; mechanical straightening is usually preferred.

Prevent First, Correct Second

Flame straightening means extra labour and risk. Reducing distortion from the start with presetting, an appropriate welding sequence, fixtures and balanced weld placement is often more economical. When straightening is needed, a dimensional check afterwards confirms that the result meets the tolerance (for example ISO 13920 classes).

Frequently Asked Questions

What is flame straightening?

A method of correcting distortion by rapidly heating a specific area of steel and using the shrinkage on cooling to bend the part back.

What temperature is used for flame straightening?

It depends on the steel. For as-rolled and normalised structural steels an upper limit of about 650 °C is generally applied; for thermomechanical and quenched steels the limit is lower. The exact value follows the producer’s recommendation and the specification.

Is the part water-cooled after straightening?

Generally not unless the specification allows it; structural steels are air-cooled. Water cooling is particularly avoided on high-strength steels.

Can stainless be flame straightened?

Yes, with care: short heating, low-carbon grades and removal of heat tint afterwards; long dwell in 450–850 °C can lower corrosion resistance.
