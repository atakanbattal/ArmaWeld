Subcontract Welding with Customer-Supplied Material: Receiving, Traceability, Scrap and Liability

2026-08-13 7 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

Some customers buy the steel themselves and leave only the welding, and where needed the coordination of cutting and other work, to the subcontractor. Set up well, this model benefits both sides; but when the owner and the processor of the material are different companies, responsibilities must be clear.

Why Customers Supply Material

  • Better steel prices on large-volume purchases,
  • Using existing stock or leftover material,
  • A required mill or origin,
  • Special grade or size,
  • Keeping steel price risk on their own side.

Receiving Inspection

When customer material arrives, the fabricator checks and records the following before production:

  • Quantity, size and weight against the delivery note,
  • Heat number and grade marking on the material matching the certificate,
  • Thickness and visual condition: rust grade, dents, scratches, transport damage,
  • Certificate type matching the order (2.2, 3.1 or 3.2).

Missing or damaged material is reported immediately with photos. A shortfall found later turns into an argument about who is responsible.

Separate Storage and Marking

Customer material should be kept apart from the fabricator’s own stock and marked with the customer’s name. If two owners’ material of the same grade and thickness gets mixed, traceability is lost. During cutting, the heat number must be transferred to cut parts and remnants (see digital traceability).

Scrap and Remnants

Cutting parts from plate always creates scrap. The contract should settle:

  • The accepted scrap allowance, or the extra quantity the customer must supply,
  • Whether usable remnants go back to the customer,
  • Who owns the scrap and whether its value is credited,
  • Who pays for material of a part spoiled by a cutting or welding error.

In practice, sharing the nesting plan with the customer shows up front how much material is needed and what will remain.

Material Defects Found During Processing

Some defects only appear during cutting or welding: laminations in the plate, thickness deviation, wrong grade or unexpected cracking in welding. Then:

  • Work stops and the customer is informed,
  • The defective area and heat number are recorded,
  • A sample is kept if needed for a claim against the mill,
  • Replacement material and extra labour cost are discussed.

Defects caused by the material are usually the responsibility of whoever supplied it; this rule should be stated in the contract.

Pricing

With customer material, the price usually covers labour, consumables (wire, gas, electrodes), cutting and other partner services, inspection and packing. Because material cost is outside the price, the fabricator carries no steel price risk, though a fee for transport, storage and handling of the material may be charged.

Lead-Time Risk

Lead time usually starts when the material reaches the fabricator. If material is short or late, the date moves. With material from several suppliers, the production plan is not final until everything has arrived (see lead-time planning).

Mixed Model

Another option: the customer supplies the main plates and sections, the fabricator sources small parts and standard materials. In that case, each item in the bill of materials (BOM) must be marked with who supplies it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who owns the scrap with customer material?

It is defined in the contract. Usually a scrap allowance is agreed; usable remnants are returned or credited.

Who is liable if the material is defective?

Defects caused by the material are usually the supplier’s responsibility; workmanship defects are the fabricator’s. The split should be in writing.

How is customer material traced?

Heat number and certificate are matched at receipt, the material is stored separately and the heat number is transferred to parts at cutting.

When does lead time start?

Usually when all material has reached the fabricator. Missing material moves the date.
