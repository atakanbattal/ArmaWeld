Steel Frames for Containers and Modular Cabins: From Base Frame to Corner Castings

2026-04-06 7 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

E-houses, equipment containers, test cabins, operator cabins and modular plant rooms are structures that are fitted with expensive equipment, then moved by truck or ship and craned into place. Their backbone is a welded steel frame. Getting that frame right determines both how easily the equipment is installed and how safely the module travels.

Main Parts of the Frame

  • Base frame: usually a welded frame of channels or I-sections; floor plate and equipment supports sit on it. It carries most of the load and has the tightest flatness expectation.
  • Columns and roof frame: carry wall and roof panels and limit twisting during lifting.
  • Corner fittings: standard container sizes use ISO corner castings; special sizes get welded lifting and lashing points.
  • Cable and pipe penetrations: cable trenches in the base, openings and reinforcements in the frame.

Dimensions and Flatness

Base frame flatness and diagonal difference matter for panel fitting and for doors that close properly. On long frames, distortion is limited by symmetrical weld sequences, pre-camber and, where needed, flame straightening (see weld distortion and flame straightening). The ISO 13920 class for general tolerances should be on the drawing (see ISO 13920 table).

Lifting and Transport

A fitted-out module sees loads the frame has never seen when it is first lifted by crane. Lifting point positions, sling angles and the loaded centre of gravity must be considered in design (see lifting lug welding). For road transport, width and height limits govern; for sea transport, lashing points and standard corner fittings do.

Corrosion Protection

Modules mostly work outdoors, sometimes in coastal or industrial atmospheres. Blasting plus a multi-coat paint system or hot-dip galvanising is chosen by corrosivity category (see ISO 12944 corrosivity categories). Inner surfaces of closed box sections and drain holes must not be forgotten.

Information for the Supplier

  • Frame drawings and equipment layout (load points).
  • Total loaded weight and centre of gravity.
  • Mode of transport (road, sea or both).
  • Corrosivity category, paint system or galvanising.
  • Inspection expectations: dimensional report, VT, MT/UT at lifting points.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most critical part of a modular cabin frame?

The base frame; it carries most of the load and has the tightest flatness requirement.

Are standard corner castings mandatory?

Usually for standard sizes and sea transport; special sizes can have welded lifting and lashing points.

How is distortion controlled on long frames?

With symmetrical weld sequences, pre-camber, fixtures and flame straightening if needed.

Which corrosion protection should be chosen?

A paint system or hot-dip galvanising matched to the ISO 12944 corrosivity category.
