Cleaning Stainless Steel Welds: Heat Tint, Pickling and Passivation

2026-04-27 6 min read ArmaWeld Engineering

Stainless steel does not rust thanks to a thin, self-forming chromium oxide layer on its surface. Welding disturbs that layer. The heat tint around the weld, from straw yellow to blue, is not just cosmetic; if not removed, it becomes the place where corrosion starts.

Why Heat Tint Is Dangerous

During welding, the surface reacts with air and forms a thickened oxide. Just beneath it remains a thin chromium-depleted layer. With reduced chromium, the surface cannot protect itself again; pitting corrosion starts at these spots, especially in chloride environments and food and chemical plants. The darker the colour (yellow, brown, blue), the thicker the oxide and the higher the risk.

Prevention: Back Purging

The best cleaning is contamination that never forms. On pipes and closed sections, the inside of the weld is protected by back purging with argon or forming gas. Inadequate purging creates “sugaring” on the root side, a porous dark oxidation that often cannot be cleaned and requires cutting out the weld. Residual oxygen after purging can be measured (see TIG welding of stainless steel).

Cleaning Methods

MethodHowAdvantage / caution
MechanicalStainless wire brush, iron-free abrasives and flap discsFast, but may not fully remove the chromium-depleted layer; can leave scratches and roughness
Pickling pastePaste containing nitric and hydrofluoric acid applied to the weld, then rinsed thoroughlyIdeal locally; chemical safety and wastewater disposal essential
Pickling bath / sprayPart immersed in a bath or solution sprayedUniform result over the whole surface; large parts need a facility
ElectrochemicalElectrolyte and current applied with a brush or padNo hydrofluoric acid, no marks; preferred on visible surfaces

Pickling removes the oxide and the chromium-depleted layer beneath it. Passivation accelerates the uniform re-formation of the protective chromium oxide on the clean surface, usually with nitric or citric acid solutions. Pickling acids also have a passivating effect. ASTM A380 (cleaning, descaling and passivation practice) and ASTM A967 (passivation treatments and verification) are common references.

Iron Contamination

Most rust stains that appear later on stainless surfaces come not from the material but from iron picked up from outside: brushes, discs, vices or forklift tines shared with carbon steel, or grinding dust from nearby work. Use separate tools and a separate work area for stainless, and handle parts so they do not touch carbon steel. Methods such as the ferroxyl test can check for iron contamination.

Acceptance and Inspection

  • Visual: the acceptable heat-tint level is defined in the specification; some sectors use colour scales (e.g. AWS D18.2).
  • Iron contamination: ferroxyl or water test.
  • Passivity: verification tests in ASTM A967 where required.
  • Records: chemical used, time, rinsing and test results go into the quality file.

Safety and Environment

Pickling chemicals, especially hydrofluoric acid, carry serious health risks. Suitable PPE, ventilation, first-aid readiness and neutralization of wastewater before disposal are mandatory. For large parts or series work, pickling is therefore done in equipped facilities.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is heat tint removed from stainless welds?

By mechanical cleaning (stainless brush, iron-free abrasives), pickling paste or bath, or electrochemical cleaning. In critical applications pickling is followed by passivation.

What is the difference between pickling and passivation?

Pickling removes the oxide and the chromium-depleted layer beneath; passivation lets the protective chromium oxide re-form on the clean surface.

Why do rust stains appear on stainless?

Usually because of iron picked up from carbon steel tools or grinding dust. Separate tools and work areas prevent this.

Why is back purging needed?

To stop the inside of the weld reacting with air and sugaring, a porous oxidation that is open to corrosion.
